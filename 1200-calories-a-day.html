Is 1,200 calories a day too little?

For almost every adult, yes. It is below what even a sedentary day burns, and NICE treats it as the upper limit of supervised low-energy diets. Here is the arithmetic, the guideline wording, and why the scale often stalls on it.

Published by SnapNutrition AI · Last updated October 17, 2026

Short answer: for almost every adult, 1,200 calories a day is a steep cut, not a normal day's intake. Resting needs alone, estimated with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, run from roughly 1,100 to 1,900 calories a day before any activity. NICE, the UK's clinical guideline body, classes 800 to 1,200 calories a day as a low-energy diet, to be considered only inside a specialist weight-management service and for no more than 12 weeks. It is not starvation by any clinical definition. And if you are eating 1,200 and not losing weight, the most likely explanation is that the real intake is higher than the log: in one small 1992 study of people in exactly that situation, it was nearly twice what they reported.

This guide is published by the team behind SnapNutrition AI, so it is not an independent review. The numbers come from the same equation the app's goal calculator uses, from the NICE guideline, and from three measurement studies linked in the sources. Where it describes a screen, that screen is ours. It answers the questions people type into search around the number 1,200, in the order they usually ask them.

Is 1,200 calories a lot to eat in a day?

No: 1,200 calories is below what almost any adult burns on a sedentary day, and for many it is below resting needs too. The starting point for that claim is resting energy expenditure, what you burn lying still for 24 hours. The usual estimate is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990, which takes weight, height, age, and sex. A daily figure then comes from multiplying by an activity band: 1.2 for a sedentary day, 1.375 for a lightly active one, higher for more. Read the last column. Even for the smallest profile, 1,200 sits under a sedentary day; for a larger man it is close to half of one. However full a 1,200-calorie plate looks, almost no adult body burns that little. It is also why the number cannot be judged on its own: the right question is how far 1,200 sits below your figure, not whether it is big or small in the abstract.

The table uses the unrounded coefficients from the 1990 paper (9.99 per kg, 6.25 per cm, 4.92 per year), as the app does; calculators that use the rounded 10 and 5 differ by a few calories. Mifflin-St Jeor is a population equation. An individual's true resting rate can differ from it by a meaningful margin, and the activity bands are coarse. Treat the table, and any app's goal, as an estimate to check against results, not a measurement.

Is 1,200 calories a day starvation?

1,200 calories a day is not starvation in any clinical sense. "Starvation" is not a term the guidelines use; what they do is sort low intakes into bands with rules attached. The current UK guideline on overweight and obesity management, NICE NG246, updated in January 2025, says:

  • 800 to 1,200 calories a day is a low-energy diet (recommendation 1.16.8). NICE says to consider it only as part of a multicomponent strategy with long-term support inside a specialist overweight and obesity management service. It applies to people living with obesity, or living with overweight and type 2 diabetes.
  • Under 800 calories a day is a very-low-energy diet (1.16.9), considered only within a specialist service for people living with obesity who have a clinically assessed need to lose weight rapidly, for example to make surgery safer.
  • Neither is a long-term strategy (1.16.10). Both must be nutritionally complete, last no more than 12 weeks, and come with ongoing clinical supervision and access to a registered dietitian (1.16.11).

So 1,200 is the top edge of a clinical band: a tool with a protocol around it, not a lifestyle target. For people losing weight without a program, the NHS's general advice is to aim for 0.5 to 1 kg a week and not to lose weight suddenly with diets.

What is a dangerously low amount of calories per day?

Below 800 calories a day is the level NICE treats as very-low-energy and allows only under clinical supervision, and for most adults any goal under their own resting needs, held for weeks, is a question for a clinician. Beyond those two markers there is no single number that is dangerous for everyone: what matters is the distance between your intake and your resting needs, how long you hold it, and your health going in. NICE requires that patients be told the risks of these diets before starting: weight cycling, weight regain, and, for very-low-energy diets, constipation, fatigue, and hair loss (1.16.12).

A practical reading for an app user: a goal that sits under your own resting figure in the table, held for more than a few weeks, is a question for a clinician before it is a target in a tracker. The same applies regardless of the number if you are under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, have a history of disordered eating, or manage a condition with medication. NICE asks clinicians to offer eating-disorder assessment where it may apply (1.16.12), for a reason. If counting has ever been a problem for you rather than a tool, Beat in the UK and NEDA in the US are the places to start.

Will I lose weight if I eat 1,200 calories a day and burn 500?

Eating 1,200 and burning 500 would mean fast weight loss on paper, but the exercise figure is often off by 20 percent or more and the intake figure is self-reported. For every profile in the table, 1,200 already sits under daily expenditure; subtract 500 calories of exercise and the net intake lands around 700, in very-low-energy territory. Three cautions before you believe the paper.

  1. The 500 is probably not 500. When Stanford researchers tested seven wrist-worn devices in 2017, no device estimated energy expenditure within 20 percent; the Apple Watch had the lowest overall error of the seven. Treat a workout's calorie figure as a rough order of magnitude; the 10,000 steps guide shows how much of a walk's total is extra.
  2. Part of it is already counted. The activity band in a calorie goal assumes some movement. Adding a workout's full burn on top of a "lightly active" goal counts the same walk twice.
  3. Both numbers are self-reported. The 1992 study described below measured people who reported exactly this pattern and found both figures badly off, the activity one by half.

The honest version: if both figures were true, the larger adults in the table would lose weight faster than the guidelines advise without supervision, and the smaller ones would sit at the top of the range. The open question is whether they are true, and the way to settle it is a careful month of complete logging, not more arithmetic.

How much weight should I lose on 1,200 calories a day?

The NHS suggests 0.5 to 1 kg a week, and a daily gap of 500 to 1,000 calories between intake and expenditure gives roughly that. How much you lose on 1,200 depends on that gap, not on the 1,200 itself. The textbook figure is about 7,700 calories per kilogram of body fat (3,500 per pound): on that arithmetic a 500-calorie daily gap is roughly 0.45 kg a week and a 1,000-calorie gap roughly 0.9 kg, so the first month comes to about 2 to 4 kg, part of it water. Two things bend the line.

  • The first week drops more. Stored carbohydrate and the water bound to it go first, so an early loss of a kilogram or two is mostly water, and the scale gives some of it back on the first high-carbohydrate day.
  • Then the rate slows. A lighter body burns less, so the same intake produces a shrinking gap. Hall and colleagues' 2011 model, which underpins the NIH Body Weight Planner, shows body weight responding to a change in intake slowly, with half-times of about a year. For anything longer than a month, use the planner rather than the 3,500 rule.

And remember what the logged number is: an estimate of intake, never a measurement of the deficit. The calorie deficit guide covers what a food log can and cannot prove.

Why can't I lose weight on 1,200 calories a day?

The most common reason is that real intake is higher than the logged 1,200. The question is old enough to have a landmark study: in 1992, researchers at Columbia University took ten obese patients with a history of "diet resistance" who reported eating under 1,200 calories a day, and measured them for 14 days with doubly labeled water, indirect calorimetry and body-composition analysis. Their energy expenditure was within 5 percent of the predicted values, so a slow metabolism was ruled out. They had underreported their food intake by an average of 47 percent and overreported their physical activity by 51 percent, with no distinct psychopathology, and they believed their obesity had a genetic cause. The paper is Lichtman et al., New England Journal of Medicine. It is a small study of a specific group, which is why the list below starts with logging and ends with medicine. The usual reasons, in order of likelihood:

  1. The 1,200 is not 1,200. Cooking oil, butter, dressings, the second helping, and the handful that is not a meal all go unlogged, and portions get read small. Photo logging does not fix this by itself: in a 2026 NIH test, four popular photo-based apps undercounted 102 weighed meals by about a third, mostly fat the camera could not see. SnapNutrition AI was not in that test, but the lesson applies to it too.
  2. The expenditure figure is an estimate too. A population equation, an activity band you picked, a watch that is off by a fifth or more. A paper deficit of 300 calories can be no deficit at all.
  3. Water is hiding the fat. Salt, carbohydrate, a hard workout, and the menstrual cycle all move scale weight by a kilogram or more, so a real loss can stay hidden for a couple of weeks. Compare weekly averages on the scale, not mornings.
  4. The log has gaps. The days people skip are rarely their lightest. An average built from five logged days out of seven is flattering by construction.
  5. A medical cause. Rarer than logging error, as the 1992 group showed, but real. If a careful month of complete logging and weekly weigh-ins shows nothing, see a doctor; thyroid function and some medicines are the usual checks.

If you want to test it, and your clinician is happy with the goal, a four-week check looks like this: log everything, including the oil; weigh the foods that carry the calories and use barcodes for packaged ones; weigh yourself at the same time each day and compare weekly averages; change nothing else. Then read the result.
