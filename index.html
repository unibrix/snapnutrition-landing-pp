Accuracy, stated plainly

That is good enough for consistent logging and not good enough for laboratory measurement, which is why every component can be edited after a scan, and why a week's trend tells you more than any single day. Frequently asked questions

What does SnapNutrition AI cost?

The app has no mandatory subscription. In BYOK mode, Gemini and OpenRouter offer free tiers; paid providers bill you directly for AI usage. Optional Auto Mode costs $2.99/month or $29.99/year after a 3-day trial. Provider pricing and free-tier availability can change.

How does the AI food scanner work?

Simply take a photo of your food, and the AI analyzes the image to identify ingredients and estimate calories, protein, carbs, and fat. You can also scan barcodes, type a description, use voice input for hands-free tracking, share a photo in from another app, or enter a meal manually when you already know the numbers.

How accurate is AI calorie counting?

AI calorie estimates are typically within 10-30% accuracy depending on portion sizes and preparation methods. SnapNutrition AI displays this disclaimer and allows you to manually adjust portions and ingredients for better accuracy. Read more about what AI calorie counters can and can't do.

Is my data private?

Yes. Your meal data stays on your device, and syncs through your own iCloud only if you turn that on. In BYOK mode, food images go straight from your device to the provider you picked — Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, or OpenRouter — and we operate no backend at all. In optional Auto Mode, photos pass through our proxy on the way to the AI and are not stored. You can export your whole diary as JSON or CSV at any time. See our Privacy Policy for full details.

What API key do I need?

Any of four: Google Gemini, OpenAI, Anthropic (Claude), or OpenRouter. Gemini is the easiest starting point because it has a free tier — create a key at aistudio.google.com, paste it in, and you can scan without paying anyone. OpenRouter also has a free tier. The app marks which providers are free while you're choosing, so you don't have to research it first.

What is BYOK (Bring Your Own Key)?

BYOK stands for Bring Your Own Key. Rather than requiring a subscription, SnapNutrition AI lets you use your own API key from Google Gemini, OpenAI, Anthropic, or OpenRouter. You pay the AI provider directly for usage — typically $0.01-0.03 per food scan, or nothing at all on Gemini's or OpenRouter's free tier. This model makes the app free to use while giving you access to the latest AI models. (For users who'd rather skip the API-key setup, an optional Auto Mode subscription is also available.) Read our full guide to the BYOK calorie tracker model.

No. Several unrelated apps and websites use a similar name, including an Android app. SnapNutrition AI is a separate app for Apple devices, made by Unibrix, and snapnutritionai.app is its only official website.

Calorie questions people ask

Three questions that come up next to calorie trackers in search, answered briefly, with the longer reads linked.

Which is the best calorie tracker?

There is no single best calorie tracker, because the apps are built for different habits. Database apps such as MyFitnessPal and Cronometer suit people who weigh ingredients and look them up. Photo-first apps such as Cal AI, SnapCalorie, and SnapNutrition AI trade some precision for speed: you point the camera, check the list of components, and fix a portion when it is wrong.

Choose by how you will actually log every day, then by what free covers in each app and where your diary is stored. SnapNutrition AI is the pick if you want photo logging on your own AI key with no subscription, a diary that stays on your device, and an Apple Watch app. The best free AI calorie tracker apps guide compares four of them line by line.

Is 1,200 calories a lot to eat in a day?

No. For almost every adult, 1,200 calories a day is a steep cut, not a lot: resting needs alone run from roughly 1,100 to 1,900 calories a day before any activity, so a 1,200-calorie day almost always sits under what even a sedentary day burns, often far under. Those resting figures come from the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, a standard formula that uses height, weight, age, and sex, and the one SnapNutrition AI's goal calculator uses.

NICE, the UK's clinical guideline body, classes 800 to 1,200 calories a day as a low-energy diet, to be considered only inside a specialist weight-management program with long-term support. Under 800 calories a day is what NICE calls a very-low-energy diet, reserved for people under specialist care with a clinical need to lose weight quickly: at that level it is a treatment, not a self-set goal. The app's calculator caps a weight-loss cut at 30 percent of your estimated expenditure and can go as low as 1,000 calories, which is inside that low-energy range, so if your goal lands near 1,200, check it with a doctor or a dietitian before going lower. The 1,200 calories a day guide works through the numbers.

Why can't I lose weight on 1,200 calories a day?

Usually because you are eating more than 1,200 calories without realizing it. Logs run low, not high: cooking oil, butter, dressings, and the second helping go unlogged, and portions get read small. In a 2026 test by researchers at the National Institutes of Health (NIDDK), four popular photo-based tracking apps undercounted 102 weighed meals by about a third, worse than the 10 to 30 percent range quoted above and all in one direction, mostly fat the camera could not see. SnapNutrition AI was not in that test, but the lesson applies to it too: the camera misses fat, so add it yourself.

What you burn is an estimate too, so a small deficit on paper can be no deficit at all. Scale weight also swings with water, salt, and glycogen (stored carbohydrate, which holds water), and that can hide a real loss for a couple of weeks. What helps: weigh the foods that carry the calories, log the oil, compare weeks rather than days, and read the 7-day average instead of one morning's number. The calorie deficit guide explains which logging errors average out and which never do. If a careful log and a flat average still disagree after a month, ask a doctor: some conditions and medicines change the picture. The 1,200 calories a day guide covers the usual reasons in order.
