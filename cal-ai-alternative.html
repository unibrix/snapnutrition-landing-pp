Cal AI Alternative for iPhone

Both apps scan your meal from a photo. The difference is what happens when the trial ends.

Last updated: August 18, 2026 · Pricing verified August 2026

Short answer: SnapNutrition AI is a Cal AI alternative for iPhone users who want photo calorie tracking without a mandatory app subscription. Cal AI's App Store listing says food-scanning results require a subscription; SnapNutrition AI instead supports BYOK provider keys and offers optional Auto Mode.

Cal AI popularised a genuinely good idea: point your camera at a plate and get calories and macros back in a couple of seconds, with none of the searching and portion-picking that made older trackers a chore. If you are reading this, you probably already believe in that idea — you tried it, it worked, and then you hit the paywall.

This page is about what else can do the same job. It is written by the people who make SnapNutrition AI, so treat it accordingly — but the comparison below is one you can verify yourself, and the "when not to switch" section is real.

The short version

Cal AI is a subscription app: the AI scanning is the product, and an active subscription is what turns it on. SnapNutrition AI does the same scanning but connects to an AI provider using your own API key. You pay the AI provider for what you use — usually a couple of dollars a month, and nothing at all if you use a provider with a free tier.

That architectural difference produces most of the others: no SnapNutrition account to create, no ads, no company server holding your food history, and no renewal date. The provider that issues your API key may require its own account.

Why "bring your own key" makes it free

A subscription tracker has to charge you more than its AI costs, because that margin is the business. BYOK removes the middle step: the app talks to the AI provider using a key that belongs to you, and you are billed by the provider at their rates. A food photo scan costs roughly $0.01–0.03 of API usage, so three meals a day lands around $1–3 per month.

The part that surprises people is that it can be zero. Google Gemini and OpenRouter both publish a free API tier. You create a key, paste it in, and scan without ever entering payment details anywhere. Free tiers are limited by rate — requests per minute and per day — rather than by a bill, and logging a handful of meals a day is nowhere near those ceilings. SnapNutrition AI marks which providers currently have a free tier while you are choosing, so you don't have to research it first.

Two honest caveats. Free tiers are a commercial decision the providers can change, and Google's free Gemini tier in particular allows human review of prompts to improve their products — if that matters for photos of your food, use a paid tier or a different provider. The full picture is in our BYOK guide.

What you get beyond the photo scan

  • Five ways in. Photo, barcode, voice, text, and manual entry for the meals you already know by heart. You can also share a photo into the app from Messages, your camera roll, or anywhere else with a share sheet.
  • A goal that's calculated, not guessed. The daily calorie target comes from your height, weight, age and activity level, and can import your profile from Apple Health instead of asking you to type it.
  • Your diary stays yours. Export everything to JSON or CSV whenever you want. Nothing is trapped behind a subscription you have to keep paying to read your own history.
  • Watch and widgets. Log by voice from your wrist; see goal progress on the Home Screen, Lock Screen, and Control Center.
  • Eleven languages, fully localised.

Honest tradeoffs — when you should NOT switch

A comparison page you can trust has to include this part:

  • Stay with Cal AI if setup friction is the dealbreaker. Creating an API key takes about five minutes even on the free path. If "subscribe and start scanning" is worth $30 a year to you, that is a perfectly rational trade and no argument here changes it.
  • Stay with Cal AI if you're on Android. SnapNutrition AI is iOS-only, and no amount of pricing advantage fixes that.
  • Neither app solves accuracy. Photo estimates are approximations for both — typically 10–30% off depending on portions, oils, and mixed dishes. That is a property of the technique, not of a particular app. Read our honest breakdown of AI calorie counter accuracy before you switch to any photo-based tracker.
  • If you want a huge verified food database rather than AI estimates, neither of these is the right tool — see our MyFitnessPal and Cronometer comparison.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a free alternative to Cal AI?

Yes. SnapNutrition AI does the same core thing — photograph a meal, get calories and macros back — without a mandatory subscription. It runs on your own AI API key, and a free-tier key from Google Gemini or OpenRouter can make AI usage cost nothing. There is no SnapNutrition account and no ads; an API provider may require its own account.

How much does Cal AI cost?

Food-scanning results require a subscription according to Cal AI's App Store listing. Available in-app purchase prices and offers vary by storefront and purchase flow, so check the actual offer instead of relying on a single quoted figure.

What does Cal AI do better?

Zero setup — subscribe and scan, with no API key to create — a very polished onboarding experience, and an Android app. SnapNutrition AI is iOS-only and asks you to paste in a key once.

Can I export my food diary?

In SnapNutrition AI, yes — the whole diary exports to JSON or CSV at any time, and your history lives on your device with optional iCloud sync rather than on a company's servers. With subscription trackers, what you can take with you depends on the vendor's export feature.

Is AI photo calorie counting accurate enough to rely on?

Any photo estimate is an approximation, typically within about 10–30% depending on portion size, hidden oils, and mixed dishes. That applies to Cal AI, SnapNutrition AI, and every other photo-based tracker equally. What matters more is being able to correct an estimate afterwards, and logging consistently enough for the trend to mean something.
